Root-Anchored Yaw Correction — project proposal · 23 slides · about 18 minutes

How to use this. The green block is what to actually say — plain language, no jargon, safe for any audience. The blue block is the precise version: use it if your audience is technical, or hold it for questions. The amber block is a question you should expect.

If you are short on time, the six slides that carry the argument are 3 (the problem), 4 (why yaw), 6 (the finding), 7 (how the phone does it), 9 (how it reaches the sensors) and 14 (the method). Everything else can be compressed.

The one sentence to land: the phone already knows which way the body is facing, the system currently ignores it, and once you fix that the remaining error is a genuine learning problem.

1Title~20 s
Say it simply

"This project is about a motion-capture suit that gets the body's shape right but points it in the wrong direction. I want to fix that using the phone that's already strapped to the hip, plus a small machine-learning model for the part the phone can't reach."

Technical

Root-anchored differential yaw correction for a magnetometer-free 15-node inertial suit, using an iPhone's visual–inertial pelvis pose as the external heading reference and a skeleton-aware temporal model for the residual per-segment yaw.

2The system~40 s
Say it simply

"Fifteen small sensors, one on each body segment. Each one figures out its own tilt and rotation. Separately, an iPhone on the hip uses its camera to work out where you are in the room. All of it streams into a browser that draws the skeleton in 3D."

Technical

ICM-20948 IMUs running the on-chip DMP in 6-axis Game Rotation Vector mode. ESP-NOW to a hub, USB to the browser. The phone runs ARKit visual–inertial odometry and joins the same stream over a WebSocket.

3The problem~45 s
Say it simply

"Here's the strange part. If you look at the skeleton on its own, it looks right — the arms and legs are in sensible positions relative to each other. But the whole body is rotated the wrong way in the room. And it slowly gets worse during a session." pause here

Technical

Root-relative pose error is small; global joint orientation error is roughly three times larger, and near-uniform across every joint including the root. That uniformity is the signature of a whole-body frame error, not per-limb estimation error.

4Why yaw is the one that breaks~75 s
Say it simply — walk the three lines on the slide

"An accelerometer measures gravity, which always points down. Now take the three ways you can rotate. Roll — tilt left or right — changes how gravity appears to the sensor, so the sensor can detect it. Pitch — tilt forward or back — also changes gravity's measured direction, so again it can detect it. Yaw — turning horizontally, like spinning on a chair — does not change the direction of gravity at all." pause "So gravity corrects roll and pitch, and tells you nothing about yaw. Without a compass, a camera, or some other outside reference, yaw drifts. That's not a bug we introduced — it's a property of the hardware."

Technical

Gravity observes two rotational DOF; heading about the gravity vector is unobservable from accelerometer and gyroscope alone. Each sensor initialises at an arbitrary heading and integrates gyro bias independently. The magnetometer is disabled by design — hard- and soft-iron distortion in the capture volume is position-dependent, so a one-time calibration does not hold.

Expect

"Why not just calibrate the magnetometer?" — Because the distortion changes as you walk around the room. And it's the constraint that makes the project interesting: most low-cost suits have exactly this problem.

5What we measured~50 s
Say it simply

"We didn't guess at this. Three things point the same way: the global error is about three times the local error; the error is spread evenly over every joint rather than concentrated in one limb; and between two recordings the correction needed flipped sign. That last one matters — it says the problem is mostly a wrong starting point, not something slowly creeping."

Technical

The ratio and the sign change are within-file, so they survive both the timing and the scale contamination in the old exports. The absolute magnitudes do not, and I'm not quoting them: the reference suit was itself inertial and shares the failure mode, and the exports carried about 7% progressive time dilation from a hardcoded frame rate.

Expect

"Why won't you give exact numbers?" — Because the only reference we had was another inertial suit with the same blind spot. Getting a proper optical reference is week 9 of the plan.

6The finding~60 s — the turn
Say it simply

"Then I read the code, and found something I didn't expect. The phone does report which way it's facing — and we throw that away. The system uses the hip sensor for direction instead, and only falls back to the phone if the hip sensor goes quiet for a full second. The phone is used for position and nothing else." pause "So the one thing in the system that actually knows which way you're facing is being ignored."

Technical

js/custom_icm.js: at line 603 the HipsAlt branch — the hip pod — writes the root's global and local orientation. At line 582 the phone branch is gated on hipsAltLast + 1000 < now, so it only runs as a timeout fallback. At line 931 the phone's px,py,pz are scaled by a constant into the root position, with no camera-to-hip lever arm, behind a truthiness guard that drops a coordinate of exactly zero.

Expect

"So is this just a bug fix?" — Stage 1 is, and I'm upfront about that. But it only fixes one segment out of fifteen. That's the next slide.

7How the phone recovers yaw~80 s
Say it simply — point at the picture first

"This is real data from NASA's Mars helicopter, and it's the same trick the iPhone uses. Two camera frames, side by side. Every coloured line joins the same physical point seen in both. That's it — that's feature tracking. The camera picks out fixed points, follows them frame to frame, and when the camera turns, all those points sweep across the image by the same angle. So you can read the rotation straight off." pause "And the key thing: the room doesn't rotate. It's an outside reference — exactly the thing a body sensor doesn't have. The gyroscope is fast but drifts; the camera is slower but stays locked to the room. Put them together and you get both. That's what ARKit does, and it's the only source of yaw anywhere in this system."

Technical

ARKit world tracking is visual–inertial odometry. Feature points are extracted and tracked across camera frames; the IMU is integrated at high rate between them. Re-observing the same static feature constrains absolute rotation, which bounds the gyro's accumulated bias error. Useful asymmetry: VIO constrains rotation considerably better than translation, and rotation is precisely what we are taking from it.

Expect

"Why can't the body sensors do this?" — No camera. Gyro and accelerometer only, so there is nothing to correct the drift against.
"Is that actually an iPhone image?" — No, and say so: it's NASA's Ingenuity helicopter, used because it's public domain and shows the mechanism clearly. Credited on the last slide.

8What that buys us — and what it doesn't~65 s
Say it simply

"Three things we get: a heading that stays put, no compass so no metal problem, and hardware already strapped to the person. But I want to be clear about the limits. It doesn't give us north — zero is just wherever you pressed start. That's actually fine for us, because we only need it to stay still, not to point anywhere in particular. It isn't always reliable: a blank wall, dim light, a covered camera, or a fast turn and it degrades, or it jumps when it re-finds itself. And it only sees the hip — it tells us nothing about the other fourteen sensors. So the phone has to report when it's struggling, and the model has to know when not to trust it."

Technical

With .gravity world alignment, Y is along gravity and the horizontal axes are fixed by the device pose at session start — so heading is session-relative with an arbitrary origin, but stable thereafter. That is sufficient: we need a consistent reference, not a geographic one. Drift is bounded, not zero, and relocalisation produces discrete jumps. ARCamera.trackingState exposes .normal, .limited(reason) and .notAvailable, with reasons including .initializing, .excessiveMotion, .insufficientFeatures and .relocalizing. Getting that state onto the wire is week 2, and it feeds the Stage 3 uncertainty gate directly.

Expect

"Doesn't it use the compass anyway?" — Only if the app asks for .gravityAndHeading, which aligns to true north via the magnetometer. If it does, that contradicts the premise of the whole project. It's an explicit week-one check in the proposal — say this, it shows you've thought about your own assumption.

9How the phone's yaw reaches the 15 sensors~70 s
Say it simply

"So how does one phone fix fifteen sensors? Four steps. The phone says where the pelvis points in the room. The suit says where it thinks the pelvis points, in its own made-up frame. Subtract them and you have the whole-body error. Then you apply that same rotation to all fifteen sensors." pause "And here's why that's legitimate: at the T-pose, every sensor was calibrated against the same suit frame. So they all inherited the same wrong starting direction. One rotation removes the part they have in common. It doesn't fix what's different between them — that's the next part of the project."

Technical

C₀ is the common-mode correction: the rotation taking the suit's root frame onto the phone's session frame. Applying it to all 15 segments is valid because sensor-to-segment calibration referenced one shared suit frame, so the initialisation offset is common. The correction is restricted to a rotation about the gravity axis, so roll and pitch — already gravity-referenced — are provably unmodified. What survives is the differential per-segment yaw, which is Stage 2.

Expect

"If they share a frame, why is there anything left?" — They share the starting error. After that each gyro drifts independently, and straps shift. The common part is what C₀ removes.

10Making the phone input usable~65 s
Say it simply

"Before any of that works, the phone's data has to be good enough to fuse, and right now it isn't. Five fixes. There's no timestamp on the phone message, so I add one and line up the clocks — there are sixteen independent clocks in this system. There's no tracking state, so I add that too, because we need to know when the phone is struggling. The camera isn't at the hip joint, so I measure the offset. There's a bug that throws away a coordinate of exactly zero. And the phone can shift on the belt, so it gets bolted to the same plate as the hip sensor." pause "None of this is machine learning. All of it has to be right first — otherwise the model gets paid to hide a calibration error."

Technical

Affine clock map per device, offset and skew. ARFrame.timestamp and ARCamera.trackingState onto the wire. Rigid phone→pelvis transform including the camera-to-hip lever arm — omitting it injects position error proportional to pelvis rotation. The position guard at custom_icm.js:931 is a truthiness test on a float. Mount slip is either eliminated mechanically or modelled as an explicitly identified state; it is not something to ask a network to absorb.

11What the model learns from the phone~75 s
Say it simply

"Now the model. It's given the fifteen orientations after the phone has anchored them, the phone's own pose and how confident it is, the skeleton layout, and the timing. It predicts fourteen leftover corrections — one per non-root sensor — and a confidence for each. It never touches the root; the phone owns that." pause "And this is the part worth understanding: the phone isn't just used once at the start — it's an input to the model. When the phone is confident, we know the root is right, so if the pose still looks wrong, the problem must be in the limbs, and the model can go fix them. When the phone is struggling, that reasoning breaks down — and the model learns to back off instead of guessing."

Technical

Inputs: 15 anchored orientations in 6D representation, phone pose and pose increments, tracking state, Δt, validity mask, skeleton adjacency. Outputs: 14 gravity-axis residuals as (sin, cos) plus a log-variance each; root residual fixed at zero. The phone's confidence conditions the attribution — it determines whether residual implausibility is identifiable as limb error at all. That is the causal link between Stage 1 and Stage 3, and it is why tracking state has to be a model input rather than just a preprocessing filter.

Expect

"Couldn't you just run the model without the phone?" — You could, but then the common and differential errors are entangled and the model has to solve both from motion alone. The phone removes one of them for free. That separation is the design.

12What could fix it~55 s
Say it simply

"I looked at the obvious options. Turn the compass on — no, that's the thing we're avoiding. A better filter on each sensor — helps a bit, but it can't invent information that isn't there. Methods that use knowledge of how joints bend — those are real and I'll use them as the bar to beat. Add a room camera — that works, but then you've bought a camera system, so I'll use that as the measuring stick, not the answer. What's left is the phone we're already wearing."

Technical

Kinematic-constraint methods (joint range-of-motion, hinge-axis estimation, zero-velocity updates) are the magnetometer-free classical baseline and are genuinely strong under informative motion. They need idealised joint models and careful gating, and degrade on static or symmetric poses. Optical capture is reserved as ground truth; an AprilTag board is held as an optional recovery anchor.

13Why both, in that order~60 s — the argument
Say it simply

"Here's the key point. The phone is on the hip, so it can tell you which way the hips are facing. It has nothing at all to say about whether the left forearm has drifted. So the phone fixes one segment and leaves fourteen. Those fourteen can only be worked out from how a human body is actually put together and how it moves — which is exactly the kind of thing you learn from data rather than write down by hand."

Technical

The phone observes the common mode. The residual is differential per-segment yaw, which is identifiable from articulated structure and motion. Fifteen orientations with mutually wrong headings produce an anatomically implausible pose; a model trained on large-scale motion capture learns that plausibility, so the corrections become the ones that make the pose look like real motion. VIP (von Marcard, ECCV 2018) optimises exactly one vertical-axis heading parameter per IMU — same variable, solved with an external camera instead.

14The method~75 s
Say it simply

"Three stages. First, use the phone to set the hips — no machine learning, just geometry. Second, a small model estimates the leftover error for the other fourteen sensors. Third — and this matters — the model also says how confident it is, so when you're standing still and there's genuinely nothing to go on, it backs off instead of making something up. And there's a checkpoint in week three: if stage one alone fixes most of it, I'll say so."

Technical

Stage 1 is a deterministic SO(3) root substitution, with a yaw-only variant compared empirically. Stage 2 predicts 14 gravity-axis residuals with the root residual fixed to zero. Stage 3 predicts heteroscedastic log-variance per sensor and gates the correction, following Kendall & Gal (NeurIPS 2017), which is also how EgoLocate and RobustCap weight visual evidence.

Expect

"What if the gate says no ML is needed?" — Then that's the finding, and it's worth reporting: it would mean the defect was a missing coordinate transform. Slide 15 covers the pivot. Say this confidently — it reads as rigour, not as hedging.

15Stage 1 — anchor the root~55 s
Say it simply

"Mechanically it's simple: work out the rotation between where the phone says the hips point and where the suit thinks they point, then apply that same rotation to everything. Two things have to be right first — the two devices need a shared clock, and we need to know exactly how the phone is mounted relative to the hip, because the camera isn't sitting at the hip joint."

Technical

Pelvis pose is the phone pose composed with the rigid phone→pelvis transform, including the camera-to-hip lever arm — omitting it injects position error proportional to pelvis rotation. Timing is an affine map per device clock, offset and skew, because there are sixteen independent clocks. Online temporal calibration (Qin & Shen, IROS 2018) shows modest offsets materially degrade visual–inertial fusion.

16Stage 2 — the model~70 s
Say it simply

"The model is deliberately small — about two hundred thousand parameters, which is tiny by modern standards. Two ideas. First, a sensor only exchanges information with its neighbours on the body: your forearm's error is constrained by your upper arm and hand, not by your opposite foot. Second, it looks at a couple of seconds at a time, because an error only becomes visible once that limb moves. And I train it on a big public motion-capture dataset with simulated sensors, so I know the exact right answer."

Technical

Graph encoder over skeleton adjacency, bidirectional GRU over a ~2 s window, per-segment head emitting (sin δψ, cos δψ, log σ²). 6D rotation representation (Zhou, CVPR 2019) for continuity; circular output avoids the ±π discontinuity. Losses: geodesic SO(3), differentiable-FK joint position, Gaussian NLL, and a smoothness term on the correction rather than on the motion. Trained on AMASS with virtual sensors; corruption distributions calibrated from instrumentation measured on the real hardware.

Expect

"Why so small? Why not a transformer?" — Because it only predicts a residual. The physics and the skeleton are given to it for free, and the real dataset is small enough that a big model would just memorise it. The correction is also locked to the gravity axis, so it structurally cannot break the roll and pitch that are already correct.

17On reinforcement learning~60 s
Say it simply

"I was asked about using reinforcement learning, so let me be straight about it. For the main job it's the wrong tool — when you already know the right answer from your training data, you should just learn it directly; reinforcement learning would throw that away and be slower. But the instinct is right, because once the suit is out in the real world there is no right answer to compare against. So there are two places it does fit: the system can keep improving itself against things it can check for free — feet shouldn't slide, joints don't bend backwards — and separately, deciding how much to trust the phone moment to moment is a real decision problem. That second one is a stretch goal."

Technical

Supervised learning dominates wherever labels exist (AMASS, optical). The deployment-time goal is better framed as self-supervised test-time adaptation (cf. Tent, ICLR 2021) against consistency signals: anchor agreement, foot-contact non-slip, joint limits, gait symmetry. The genuinely RL-shaped sub-problem is a trust-gating policy over phone vs suit, which is a contextual bandit over a handful of discrete trust levels with reward = negative drift proxy.

Expect

"So you're not doing RL?" — Not for the core, and I'd rather say that now than defend a weaker result later. The adaptation piece delivers what RL was wanted for.

18How we'll know it worked~55 s
Say it simply

"I measure heading error in degrees per body part, and also where the joints end up in space, because a small error at the hips becomes a big error at the hand. Three rules I'm setting in advance: I pick the baseline to beat before I look at the test data; I train and test on different people; and I won't claim real-world accuracy without a proper optical system — comparing against another inertial suit proves nothing, because it has the same blind spot."

Technical

Primary: per-segment gravity-axis heading error, SO(3) geodesic error, and global joint-position RMSE after forward kinematics, with session-level paired bootstrap CIs. The strongest baseline is selected on validation and frozen before the test set is opened. Splits are subject-disjoint. Exactly one optical-to-session registration at initialisation — per-frame alignment would erase the phenomenon being measured.

19Ten weeks~50 s
Say it simply

"Ten weeks. The first two are unglamorous — reading the phone code and fixing the logging — and that's on purpose. The risky question is whether the phone actually sends what I need, and I want to find that out in week one, not week seven. Week three is the checkpoint. Then data and baselines, then the model, then real recordings, then writing."

Technical

Weeks 1–3 are the dependency-critical path: message schema, frame conventions, timestamps and tracking state, clock map, phone→pelvis calibration, then the deterministic Stage 1 measurement. Weeks 4–8 are self-contained and need only the repository and a laptop. Weeks 9–10 depend on hardware and optical access, which are the items most likely to slip.

20The honest risk~45 s
Say it simply

"The honest risk is that the geometry fix alone solves most of it and there isn't much left for the model to do. I'd rather say that now than discover it in week nine. And it wouldn't be a failed project — it would be a result: it'd show the problem was a missing coordinate transform, not something that needed a neural network. The checkpoint is early enough that I can pivot to a related problem on the same data."

Technical

Pivot targets on the same pipeline: packet-loss reconstruction on the SO(3) manifold (labels free by masking, gap distributions already measured), or tracking-quality classification from the stream and the instrumentation counters. Both are self-contained and need no additional hardware.

Expect

"Isn't that a lot of risk for a graded project?" — That's why the gate is week 3 and why the pivot targets are already scoped. Either path ends in a complete, evaluated result.

21What this contributes~45 s
Say it simply

"Four things. A working way to anchor this kind of suit to a phone's camera tracking. A small model that fixes what's left once that anchor exists. A test setup that can actually tell the different causes apart, which we can't do today. And evidence about when a plain geometric fix is enough versus when learning the body's structure genuinely adds something. That last one is useful to anyone building a cheap motion-capture suit."

Technical

The benchmark separates initialisation offset, common yaw drift, differential per-segment drift, tracking reset, mount slip and timing error — currently confounded. The generalisable claim is the deterministic/learned boundary for magnetometer-free suits with a body-worn visual anchor.

23Questions—
Say it simply

"Happy to take questions."

Have ready

"How is this different from existing IMU pose papers?" — Those infer pose from few sensors. I have plenty of sensors; I'm resolving one unobservable direction across all of them.
"Why not just use the phone's compass?" — Worth checking. If the app requests heading-aligned tracking it is using the magnetometer, which would contradict the premise. That's a week-one question in the proposal.
"How much data do you need?" — Training is on a large public dataset with simulated sensors, so that's not the constraint. The constraint is real recordings with an optical reference.
"Will it run in real time?" — The model is small enough to, but the graded version is offline because a bidirectional model over a short window is more accurate. Real time is a stretch goal.
